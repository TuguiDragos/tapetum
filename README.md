The *tapetum lucidum* is the mirror behind the retina that makes a cat's eyes
flare in the dark. The same structure sits in almost every nocturnal animal, and
each one throws back a different colour: green in cats, blue in horses, red in
alligators, white in spiders. One mechanism, a whole collection of colours.

That is how these are built. 4 families, each traced back to something that
makes light rather than reflects it, and each in a dark and a light variant.

## Quantum

<p align="center"><img src="https://raw.githubusercontent.com/TuguiDragos/tapetum/main/readme-assets/tapetum-quantum-dark.png" alt="Quantum" width="900" /></p>
<p align="center"><img src="https://raw.githubusercontent.com/TuguiDragos/tapetum/main/readme-assets/tapetum-quantum-light.png" alt="Quantum Light" width="900" /></p>

A nebula is not lit. It glows.

A star nearby strips the electrons off its hydrogen, and when those electrons
fall back they hand the energy over as a photon at one exact wavelength. Nothing
is illuminating the cloud. The gas is doing it, and it only knows a handful of
colours, always the same ones.

Violet keywords, teal strings, a blue that sits deep enough to read for hours.

## Fraunhofer

<p align="center"><img src="https://raw.githubusercontent.com/TuguiDragos/tapetum/main/readme-assets/tapetum-fraunhofer-dark.png" alt="Fraunhofer" width="900" /></p>
<p align="center"><img src="https://raw.githubusercontent.com/TuguiDragos/tapetum/main/readme-assets/tapetum-fraunhofer-light.png" alt="Fraunhofer Light" width="900" /></p>

In 1814 a Bavarian glassmaker pointed a prism at the sun and found the spectrum
was not continuous. Dark lines cut through it, hundreds of them, in the same
places every time. Joseph von Fraunhofer labelled the strongest A through K and
published, without knowing what they were.

They were elements. Sodium in the sun's atmosphere swallowing light at 589 nm,
precisely where sodium would emit it if you heated it yourself. He had found a
way to read the composition of a star from the light it sends.

Every colour here is one of those lines, converted from its wavelength through
the CIE 1931 observer. Mercury at 435.8. H-beta at 486.1. The forbidden oxygen
line at 495.9 that was blamed on an invented element called nebulium for 60
years. Sodium at 589. H-alpha at 656.3.

## Persistence

<p align="center"><img src="https://raw.githubusercontent.com/TuguiDragos/tapetum/main/readme-assets/tapetum-persistence-dark.png" alt="Persistence" width="900" /></p>
<p align="center"><img src="https://raw.githubusercontent.com/TuguiDragos/tapetum/main/readme-assets/tapetum-persistence-light.png" alt="Persistence Light" width="900" /></p>

A phosphor screen does not hold a picture. It fades, and how it fades was an
engineering decision.

P1 zinc silicate flares green and is gone in milliseconds, which is what you want
on an oscilloscope where the trace must not smear. P7 is 2 layers: a blue flash
as the beam lands, then a yellow-green afterglow that outlives the sweep. Radar
operators needed that. The beam moves on, and the target stays on the glass long
enough to be believed.

The accents are those phosphors. The ground is the screen before the beam
arrives, which is not black but a warm, dead green.

## Anodise

<p align="center"><img src="https://raw.githubusercontent.com/TuguiDragos/tapetum/main/readme-assets/tapetum-anodise-dark.png" alt="Anodise" width="900" /></p>
<p align="center"><img src="https://raw.githubusercontent.com/TuguiDragos/tapetum/main/readme-assets/tapetum-anodise-light.png" alt="Anodise Light" width="900" /></p>

Titanium has no colour worth mentioning. Grey, faintly warm, that is all.

Put it in an acid bath and raise the voltage, and an oxide layer grows on the
surface a few atoms at a time. Light bouncing off the top of that layer meets
light bouncing off the metal underneath, and depending on how far apart they are
some wavelengths cancel and one survives. 30 volts is blue. 50 is gold. 60, magenta. 70, teal.

No pigment is involved. Nothing was added. The colour is the thickness, and you
set it with a dial.

## How they are checked

Colour is a judgement call, so every value here is placed by hand. An earlier
version let an optimiser choose them and it converged: 2 of the families came
out under 2 dE apart, and it had bleached the keywords to near grey chasing its
own objective.

What is measured, on every variant:

- **Contrast.** Every syntax colour against its own background, and every piece
  of interface text against the surface it actually sits on. Syntax clears
  4.5 to 1, comments clear 4.0.
- **Separation.** CIEDE2000 between every pair of coloured roles. The closest
  pair in any theme is 12.5.
- **Coverage.** All 729 colour keys registered in the running VS Code build,
  including the chat, agents, inline edit and modern tab surfaces that most
  themes leave to the defaults.

The checks are not a hand-written list. `tools/extract-pairs.mjs` reads the
workbench stylesheet out of the installed editor and collects every foreground
and background used in the same rule, so what gets checked is what VS Code
actually paints together. `tools/extract-derivations.mjs` reads the binary for
the keys whose default is another key, which catches the class of bug where a
value contradicts the surface beneath it.

```bash
node tools/analyze.mjs    # all 8, against the real pairs
node tools/compare.mjs    # against every theme Microsoft ships
```

6 roles carry their own hue: keywords, strings, functions, types, numbers and
tags. Parameters and properties take the foreground colour and are told apart by
italic, because a 7th saturated hue cannot be held away from the other 6
without washing all of them out.

## Customizing

Override anything per theme in your `settings.json`:

```jsonc
{
  "workbench.colorCustomizations": {
    "[Tapetum Quantum]": {
      "editor.background": "#080B14"
    }
  },
  "editor.tokenColorCustomizations": {
    "[Tapetum Quantum]": {
      "comments": "#7580AD"
    }
  }
}
```
